# The Recording Habit

I record every consult. It is the single fastest skill jump available to a photographer who sells on the phone, and it costs nothing but the first awkward sentence. This section covers how I ask, how I listen back, how I write the proposal off the transcript, and how the questions couples ask become the posts I publish.

## Asking for consent

I use [your recording tool], a notetaker that joins the video call as a participant so the couple can see it. If you run calls by phone, your phone can record with an app and the same sentence applies. Either way, I say it before anything else of substance:

"Just so you know, I record these calls so I can send you a proper summary afterwards instead of scribbling notes while you talk. Is that alright with you?"

Nobody has ever said no. The reason is in the sentence. The recording is for them. It means I can look at them and listen instead of typing. If a couple did say no I would stop the recorder, say thank you, and take notes the old way. Consent is not a formality. Where I live the law requires it. [Check the recording consent rule where you are.] Ask regardless, because the couple deserves it.

Tell them again if someone new joins the call late. Partners drop in halfway through all the time.

## Listening back once

Within 24 hours I listen to the call once, at one and a half speed. Not to relive it. To score it. I keep the scorecard short and I answer each line yes, partly or no:

- Did I find out why they reached out to me specifically?
- Did I learn what they want the photographs to feel like, in their words?
- Did I reflect their priorities back and get a yes?
- Did I learn the parts of the day that could change my recommendation?
- Did I avoid asking things they had already told me in the enquiry?
- Did I listen more than I talked?
- Did I recommend, rather than list options?
- Did I ask what "think about it" actually meant?
- Did I agree the next touchpoint before we hung up?

Then I write one improvement for the next call. One. Not five. The first time I did this I discovered I was answering my own questions before the couple could. The second time I discovered I said "yeah" about ninety times. Pick the single thing that would have changed the outcome most and fix that one on the next call.

Three or four calls in, patterns show up that you would never catch live. You will hear where the couple went quiet. You will hear the question they asked twice because you did not answer it the first time. You will hear the moment they decided, which is almost never where you thought it was.

## Writing the proposal off the transcript

The proposal is a note from me, not a quote. The transcript is how I make it sound like one.

Before I write, I search the transcript for five things:

1. The personal detail. Where they proposed, the thing they laughed about, the venue they chose and why. One of these opens the email.
2. Their words for the photography. "Candid." "Not a production." "Editorial but still us." I use their words, not mine.
3. The coverage we agreed and why. The getting-ready locations, the second photographer decision, the finish time they chose. The proposal repeats the reasoning as well as the number.
4. Anything I promised to send. Vendor recommendations, a gallery link, a check on a videographer's availability. If I said it, it goes in the email or I lose trust.
5. Anything still open. Ceremony time not confirmed, a tea ceremony they are deciding on. I name it so they know the plan can flex.

The proposal then writes itself in about twenty minutes. Opening with the personal detail. One paragraph of what I recommended and why, in their language. The draft timeline from the call, pasted in plain text. The next step. A line saying I will check in on a named day if that is alright with them. Deliverables as dot points, with the exact gallery delivery date worked out from [your delivery timeframe], never "six to eight weeks".

If the notetaker produces a summary, use it as a checklist, not as copy. Summaries flatten everyone into the same voice. The point of the transcript is that it preserves theirs.

One more thing the transcript does. If a couple pushes on price, I copy their exact sentence into a log with the date. Never paraphrased softer or harder than they said it. Ten of those lines tell me more about my pricing than any amount of thinking does, and the pricing section explains how I read them.

## Mining the call for content

Every consult is a content session the couple paid for with their attention. Three things come out of each one.

The questions. Couples ask the same things in different words. "How many photos do we get?" "Are they all edited?" "Do we get a sneak peek?" "What if it rains?" "Do you help with the timeline?" Each question is a post, a reel script, a frequently asked questions entry on your website and a line in your next enquiry reply. I keep a running list with a tally. The question that has been asked most this quarter is the one I film next.

The answers. The way you explain something to a nervous couple at 7pm on a Tuesday is your voice. It is warmer and plainer than anything you would write cold. When I need a caption or a script, I search the transcripts for the time I explained that thing well and I start from what I actually said.

The moments. "Would you be happy to end around the first dance? After ten o'clock it's evidence, not photography." A couple laughed at that on a call. It became a post about coverage length that did better than anything I had written about packages. The lines that land live are the lines that land on screen.

Never quote a couple by name. Never post anything they would recognise as theirs without asking. The pattern is yours. The specifics are not.

## The habit, in one line

Record with consent. Listen back once and fix one thing. Write the proposal in their words. Turn their questions into your next post. Four steps, maybe forty minutes a consult, and the compounding is faster than anything else in this business.
