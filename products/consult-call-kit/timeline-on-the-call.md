# The Timeline on the Call

Most photographers run a consult as an interview followed by a price. I run it as a planning session. Somewhere around minute eight I share my screen, type the couple's wedding date and ceremony time into my timeline builder, and build their day in front of them. That ten minutes does three jobs at once. It is my discovery. It is my recommendation. And it is the moment the couple stops interviewing a photographer and starts planning a wedding with one.

This section gives you the short version for the call. The full pre-wedding timeline process, the one I run [four to six weeks] before the day, is a separate piece of work and is pointed to at the end.

## Why this works

A couple at the consult stage has booked a venue and maybe a celebrant. They have no idea how their day will actually run. They are nervous about it. When I show them that a 1pm ceremony gives them two portrait locations and an hour to rest before they walk into the reception, I have solved a problem they did not know how to solve. I did it for free, in real time, with no sales language.

It also answers the question they came to ask me. The coverage hours fall out of the timeline. I never have to say "most couples take the ten hour package". I say "your day runs from 10 in the morning to 10 at night, so that is what I would cover", and the couple watched me work it out. Nobody argues with arithmetic they did themselves.

The third job is quieter. Building the timeline is how I find the facts I need. Where each of them is getting ready. How far apart those two places are. How big the bridal party is. Whether there is a tea ceremony, a church with a hard finish, a cultural morning that starts at seven. Each of those changes the recommendation, and each one surfaces naturally when I ask "what time do you want to be at the church?" rather than "how many hours do you want?"

## What I say to open it

Once I have the ceremony time I stop them. "Let me share my screen before you go any further. I've got a little tool I use for this and it'll save you about three hours of work. I'll send it to you afterwards whether you book me or not."

That last sentence matters. It tells them the next ten minutes are a gift, not a pitch. It also means I can be honest about the day without them wondering what I am selling.

## The six inputs

I need six things to build a draft that holds up. I ask for them in roughly this order. Some they know, some they guess, some I fill in from experience and say so.

1. The date. This gives me sunset, and sunset sets the back half of the day.
2. The ceremony time and how long it runs. A civil ceremony is 20 to 30 minutes. A full church service is 45 to 60. A cultural ceremony can run longer. [Write in the ceremony lengths you see most where you shoot.] If I do not know, and I say "I'll put down an hour for now and we can tidy it later."
3. Where the ceremony and reception are, and the travel time between them.
4. Where each of them is getting ready, and how far apart those two places are. This is the single input that decides whether the day needs two photographers.
5. Bridal party size. Big groups move slowly. Every location change with eighteen people costs more time than the couple expects.
6. Anything happening before the ceremony or after the entrance that must be photographed. A tea ceremony. A groom's shaving. A first dance they care about. A send-off.

If they do not know an answer I do not stall. I put in a sensible number, say "we can always adjust this later", and keep moving. The draft only needs to be close enough to show the shape of the day.

## The blocks I build, in order

I build from the ceremony outwards, then check the whole thing against sunset.

After the ceremony. Fifteen to thirty minutes of congratulations, because people will hug them whether or not it is on the timeline. Then family and friend portraits straight away, while everyone is still in one place. Then ten minutes for the two of them alone, because it is a real wedding and they have just been married.

Portrait locations. Travel time to each one, plus a buffer. One hour at each location. Travel back. I say the buffer out loud: "I give every car trip [ten] minutes on top of the drive time, because parking, gear and finding the spot always eat it." Couples relax when they hear that. It tells them I have done this before.

The rest window. If the day allows it, an hour before the reception entrance to sit down, touch up and breathe. I point at it and say "you get an hour to rest here". That hour is often the thing they remember from the call.

Sunset. The light decides when portraits should end, and therefore when the entrance should be. If the entrance is set too early for the sunset, I say so and suggest moving it. Venues rarely mind thirty minutes.

The morning. I work backwards from the ceremony. Travel to the ceremony with a buffer. Final touches and getting into the dress or suit. Hair and makeup, roughly an hour each per person being styled, more for a big party. That gives me a start time for the first photographer and tells me when the second one is needed on the other side of town.

The evening. Entrance, speeches, cake, first dance, and anything cultural. I ask what matters most and where they would be happy for coverage to end. "Most couples finish me around the first dance. Some want the dance floor. After about ten o'clock it is evidence, not photography." They laugh, and they pick.

## Letting the hours fall out

Once the blocks are in place I read the day back. "So that is 10am to 10pm, about twelve hours. The second photographer starts with you at 11 and finishes when you enter the reception at 6.30, so about seven and a half hours." Then I stop and ask how it looks.

This is where the recommendation lives. I do not present packages. I say what the day needs and why, using the facts we just put on the screen together. If two photographers make sense I say exactly which block made them make sense: "You're getting ready 45 minutes apart, so one photographer would miss half of one of you." If the day is small and close together I say one photographer is enough, and I say it before they ask. Telling a couple they need less than they expected is the fastest trust I know how to build.

If the day is still fluid, which it usually is twelve months out, I build the longest realistic version and name it as a ceiling. "This is the maximum. Everything we tighten from here brings the number down, never up." That one sentence removes the fear that the price will grow after they book.

## What I send afterwards

The same day, the couple gets the draft timeline, a short summary of what we discussed and the proposal. The timeline goes in the proposal email as plain text they can forward to their planner, their parents and their hair and makeup artist. I tell them to send it back when vendors firm up and I will lock the changes in.

The draft does not need to be perfect. It needs to be theirs, built with them, in a form they can share.

## If you do not have a timeline tool

Build one. A spreadsheet with a column of times and a column of events is enough to start. The rules the tool needs are the ones above: ceremony length, 15 to 30 minutes of congratulations, 30 minutes of family portraits, 10 minutes alone, travel plus a 10 minute buffer every car trip, one hour per portrait location, an hour of rest if the day allows, entrance set against sunset, and the morning worked backwards from the ceremony at about an hour per person for hair and an hour for makeup. Look up the sunset for the date before the call, from [your sunset source]. Never guess it.

The tool is not the point. Building the day in front of them is the point. A pen and a printed grid would do the same job, slower.

## The full process this points to

What I build on the call is the draft. [Four to six weeks] before the wedding I run the full pre-wedding timeline process with the couple: confirmed vendor times, exact addresses, the family photo list, a named person to round people up for portraits, and the back-to-back check with the church or venue. That process is its own tool in the program. The call version exists to earn the booking and to give the couple something useful on the way out, whichever way they decide.
