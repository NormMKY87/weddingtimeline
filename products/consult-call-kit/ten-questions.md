# Ten Questions That Surface Budget and Decision Makers

I never ask a couple what their budget is. I never ask who makes the decision. Both questions put people on guard, and both get answers that are polite rather than true. Instead I ask about the day. The budget and the decision makers surface on their own, inside answers the couple is happy to give, and I listen for them.

Each question below does honest work on its own. Each one also carries a tell. The tell is what I am listening for underneath. Do not ask all ten. Pick the ones the enquiry has not already answered, and let the rest come up while you build the timeline.

## 1. "Where are you both getting ready?"

Why it works. It is the most natural planning question there is, and it decides whether the day needs two photographers.

The tell. Listen for the conditional. "A hotel suite if the budget allows." "Probably at home, we're trying to keep costs down." "We've booked the penthouse." Couples tell you where they sit financially in the way they talk about the morning. Listen also for who decided. "Her mum wants us at the house" means a parent is in the room for this wedding, and possibly paying for it.

What I do with it. Build the morning. Decide the second photographer on distance. File the budget signal without commenting on it.

## 2. "What have you locked in so far, and what's next on the list?"

Why it works. It tells me where photography sits in their priorities and how far along they are.

The tell. If I am third after venue and celebrant, photography matters to them and they have budgeted for it. If I am eighth after the band and the cake, I am a line item. Listen for the planner. "Our planner said to sort photography now" means a third decision maker who will read the proposal before they do. Listen for the money words: "we've paid the venue deposit", "we're waiting on the parents for the rest".

What I do with it. Match the pace of the call to where they are. A couple twelve months out gets a ceiling and a draft. A couple four months out gets a recommendation and a date to decide by.

## 3. "What made you reach out to me specifically?"

Why it works. It tells me what they already value, in their words, so I can reflect it back instead of guessing.

The tell. Referral is the biggest. "Our friends had you" means they have already seen a price and a gallery and chosen to call anyway. That is a couple who can afford me and has half decided. "We found you on Instagram and loved the editing" means style is the criterion and price is still an open question. "Our venue recommended you" means the venue will hear how this goes.

What I do with it. Honour the referral by name if they give one. Use their words for my work in the recommendation. Note who else is listening.

## 4. "What feeling are you getting from your parents? Traditional, or low key?"

Why it works. For many couples the day is shaped as much by parents as by the couple, and asking about the feeling rather than the money lets them tell me either way.

The tell. "Mum's quite traditional" is a decision maker. "Dad's paying for the reception so he has opinions" is a decision maker with a budget. "They're leaving it to us" tells me the couple is both deciding and paying, and I should expect them to decide together, slowly, and on their own money.

What I do with it. If a parent is paying or deciding, I make sure the proposal reads well to someone who was not on the call. Plain language, the timeline attached, the reasoning for each decision written down. I may suggest the couple share the draft timeline with the parent before anything else, because a parent who sees the day planned relaxes about the photographer.

## 5. "How big is your bridal party?"

Why it works. Movement. Eighteen people take longer to get anywhere than six, and that changes the timeline and the second photographer decision.

The tell. Scale is a budget proxy. A large party at a city hotel with a church ceremony and two portrait locations is a wedding with a large guest list and a large spend, and photography will be sized to match. A party of two and a small ceremony in a park tells me to lead with a shorter day and say so before they ask.

What I do with it. Build travel and buffer time to suit. Recommend the day that fits the scale, in both directions.

## 6. "Are you exploring a videographer?"

Why it works. It is helpful. I can recommend good ones, I can check their availability, and I need to know for the timeline.

The tell. A booked videographer means the couple has already spent at a known level on an adjacent service, and photography usually sits at or above it. "Not yet, we're starting with photos" tells me photography is first and the whole budget is still intact. "We might just get a friend to film" tells me they are trimming.

What I do with it. Recommend, offer to check availability, and shape the recommendation to what the video tells me about the day.

## 7. "Is it a full day, or is there a part of the day that matters most to you?"

Why it works. It asks for priorities, which is what I need to recommend well.

The tell. The way they answer the first half is the budget tell. "Oh, the whole thing, start to finish" is a couple who expects to pay for a full day. "Mostly the ceremony and the portraits" is a couple who has either a smaller budget or a clear sense of what they value, and either way the right answer is a shorter day. The second half tells me what they will fight to keep when hours are trimmed.

What I do with it. Build the timeline to match, and name the coverage that falls out of it.

## 8. "Have you been to a wedding recently that you loved, or one that showed you what you don't want?"

Why it works. It is the kindest way to learn their taste and their fears, and it never asks them to criticise a photographer by name.

The tell. "My sister's photographer made everyone pose for two hours" is a fear I can answer with how I run portraits. "Our friends got their photos eight months later" is a fear I answer with a delivery date. "Our friends' wedding was beautiful but they spent a fortune" is the budget tell, and I hear it without responding to it. Listen also for who was at that wedding with them. "Mum hated the photographer" tells me who will have a view on mine.

What I do with it. Address the fear directly in the recommendation. It is the most persuasive thing I can do.

## 9. "Am I the first photographer you've spoken to, or have you got a few lined up?"

Why it works. It is honest and it is disarming. Couples expect photographers to avoid this question, and asking it plainly earns trust.

The tell. "You're the first" means I am setting the anchor for everything they see next, and I should make the recommendation and the timeline count. "We've spoken to three" means they have a price range in their head already, and if they are still on the call with me the range includes me. "We've spoken to a few and you're the most expensive" is price pushback arriving early, and I take it to the objections section.

What I do with it. If I am first, I give them the questions to ask the others. If I am last, I ask what has made comparing difficult so far.

## 10. "What would you both want to feel clear on before you decide?"

Why it works. It is the decision maker question, asked without asking it. It names the decision as joint, it gives the quiet partner a reason to speak, and it tells me what is unresolved.

The tell. If one partner answers for both, that is the decision maker, and the other partner's silence is what I need to address before the call ends. "I'd want to run it past my dad" is a third decision maker named out loud. "Honestly the only thing is the number" is the budget, delivered without me asking for it. "Nothing, we're pretty sure" is a couple who is ready, and I should ask for the decision.

What I do with it. Answer what can be answered now. Offer a short second call if a partner or parent needs to meet me. Agree the next touchpoint.

## A note on the order

Questions one, two and five come up while I build the timeline and need no set-up. Three and eight belong in discovery, before the screen share. Four and six fit anywhere planning is being discussed. Seven is the bridge from the timeline into the recommendation. Nine and ten belong at the end, after the recommendation, when the couple is deciding.

## How these were tested

I checked each question against three recorded consults from my own calendar before writing it down. Every tell above appeared in at least one of those calls without the couple being asked for it directly. A couple told me their getting-ready plan depended on the budget inside the first five minutes. Another named the parents' expectations the moment I asked about tradition. A third told me I was the first photographer they had spoken to, unprompted, when I asked how the number felt.

Run the list on your next live consult, with [your own questions] added where they fit. Tick the questions you asked, circle the tells you heard, and note which ones you missed. After three calls you will know which four or five do the most work for the way you sell.
