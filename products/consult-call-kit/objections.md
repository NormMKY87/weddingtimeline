# The Objections, in Depth

The Booking Pack carries the short replies you can paste into a message. This is the long version, the one for the call itself, where you have a voice and a face and a couple who can hear whether you mean it.

## Objections are information

Before any technique, the stance. An objection is not a wall between me and a booking. It is the couple telling me what they need to know or believe before they can decide. My job on the call is to find that out and answer it honestly. Sometimes the honest answer is that I am not the right fit, and a clean no is a successful call.

The numbers back the stance. If I close fewer than three consults in ten, objection handling is not my problem. The offer is wrong, or the wrong people are reaching the call, and no reply will fix that. If I close more than seven in ten, my prices are too low and the next move is a rise, not a celebration. The band in between is where selling skill matters at all. So I count. Consults held, bookings made, and every price objection written down in the couple's own words with the date. Ten of those lines tell me more than a month of worrying.

Two more distinctions I hold onto. An obstacle comes up before I have made a recommendation, and I can usually clear it with a question or a fact. An objection comes up after I have asked for the decision, and it needs diagnosis before it needs an answer. And the first reason a couple gives is often not the real one. "We need to think about it" can mean money, fit, a partner, a parent, a bad experience with a photographer at a sibling's wedding, or simple exhaustion with planning. I do not solve the first thing I hear. I ask.

## The three moves

Every reply below runs the same three moves. Acknowledge, associate, ask.

Acknowledge. Say back what they said, calmly, so they know it landed. "That makes sense. It is a significant investment and I would never expect you to decide it lightly."

Associate. Normalise it, but only if that is true. "A lot of couples find it hard to compare photographers when the inclusions look the same on paper." Never "serious couples invest more", never "you only get married once", never anything that trades on status or shame. If you cannot normalise it honestly, skip this move.

Ask. One targeted question that finds the real concern. Then stop talking and let them answer.

Once I know the real concern I answer it, check whether that resolved it, and if fit is still clear I ask for the decision once more. If a new concern appears I run the three moves again. If they decline or ask for space, I stop closing and I mean it.

## 1. Price pushback

What it sounds like: "That's more than we were expecting." "Why are you more than the others we've spoken to?" "That's a lot."

What it usually means. One of two things, and they need different answers. Either the total is outside what they are comfortable spending, which is affordability. Or they can spend it but are not yet convinced the difference is worth it, which is value. Most photographers hear the first and answer the second, with a list of inclusions. That makes it worse.

The question: "When you say it feels like a lot, is it more that the total is outside what you'd be comfortable spending, or that you're not yet sure the difference is worth the extra?"

If it is value, I go back to what they told me mattered in discovery and I explain the difference through that, not through features. Two pillars at most. "You told me the thing you were most worried about was the day turning into a photoshoot. The second photographer is what lets me stay with you while the other side of the morning still gets covered. That is most of the difference between the two options you're looking at." Then one piece of real proof if I have it. A gallery from a day shaped like theirs. A story about a couple with the same worry.

If it is comparison, I ask: "Apart from price, what feels different between the two options?" and if they are not sure: "If both were the same investment, which would you feel more confident choosing, and why?" The answer tells me whether the real decision is style, trust, coverage or price. I never criticise the other photographer and I never guess why they are cheaper.

If it is affordability, I go to the budget section below.

What I never do: defend the price with an inclusion list, discount before I have diagnosed, or keep closing after the real answer is that they cannot spend it.

## 2. The budget question

This one has two faces and I handle them differently.

The first face is a couple asking the price early, before I have built the timeline. I answer. A starting figure and the typical range, in one sentence, then one helpful question. "Full day commissions with two photographers typically sit in [your range], and the main thing I'd want to understand before pointing you to a number is how your day is shaped and whether a second photographer actually earns its place. Where are you both getting ready?" Answer first, then ask. Never force a couple through a call before they get a number. It reads as hiding and it is.

The second face is a couple naming a budget that is under my floor. "We've got about [X] set aside for photography." This is affordability, and I respect it as a real boundary. I do not pretend it is a value problem, and I do not find them finance.

The question: "What were you hoping that would include?" Often the answer is a shape of day that does not need what they think it needs. A couple who assumed they needed ten hours and two photographers may have a six hour day with everyone in one building. Then the honest route is to change the scope. Shorter coverage, one photographer, a smaller deliverable. I rebuild around what matters most to them and I quote that.

If the scope cannot move and the number cannot either, I have two honest options left. If I work with other photographers whose rates sit where the couple is, [name yours], I introduce one properly, by name, as a recommendation and not a consolation. If I do not, I say so kindly and leave the door open. "I'd rather tell you straight than squeeze a day I can't do properly. If anything changes, you know where I am."

I never ask a couple for their budget. The ten questions exist so I can hear it without asking.

## 3. Still deciding

What it sounds like: "We need to think about it." "We want to speak to a few more people." "Can we come back to you?"

What it usually means. Something is unresolved and they do not yet have the words for it, or they are simply not ready and need the permission to say so.

The question: "Of course. What are the main things you want to think through?" Asked with real curiosity, not as a trap. If they can name it, I now have an obstacle to clear, and I clear it. If they cannot, I offer the menu: "Is it mainly the investment, the fit, the coverage, or are you just not ready to decide yet?" Any of the four is a fine answer. "Not ready" is a fine answer.

Then I make the decision easier to make, not harder to avoid. I summarise what we agreed in two sentences. I tell them exactly what they will receive from me and when. I say what is true about the date. If the date is one that fills early I say so. If it is not, I do not invent a deadline. Manufactured urgency costs more trust than it earns, and a wedding date is real enough on its own.

Finally I book the next touchpoint before we hang up. Never "let me know". Always a day. "I'll send the proposal and the timeline tonight. Would it help if I checked in on Thursday after you've both had a look?" They almost always say yes, and now the follow-up is something they asked for.

## 4. The partner wants someone else

What it sounds like: "My partner really liked another photographer." "I love your work but [partner] is leaning a different way." Sometimes it arrives by message after the call, from the one who was not on it.

What it usually means. A joint decision with two sets of criteria, and the couple is working out how to reconcile them. It is almost never a verdict on you.

What I never do: tell one partner to overrule the other, frame the decision as support versus permission, or encourage a booking one half of the couple does not want. A wedding photographed for a couple where one of them wanted someone else is a bad day for everyone.

The question: "What was it about their work that [partner] responded to?" The answer is useful whichever way it goes. If it is a style I do not shoot, I say so and wish them well. If it is something I do and simply did not show, I can show it. If it is a feeling rather than a style, I ask the second question: "What would [partner] need to feel clear on before you both decide?"

Then the offer: a short second call with both of them, fifteen minutes, specifically so the absent partner can meet me. Most of these objections dissolve when the second person hears the plan for the day from the person who will be running it. If a second call is not possible I send a short written recap aimed at the partner, in plain language, with the timeline attached, and I agree a day to check in.

## 5. Fewer hours

What it sounds like: "Can we do it with less coverage?" "Do we really need the morning?" "What if we just do the ceremony and portraits?"

What it usually means. Either the number is too high and they are looking for a lever, or they do not yet see what the hours buy them. Sometimes they are right and the day is shorter than I drafted.

The question: "Which parts of the day are most important for you to have fully documented?" I go back to the timeline on the screen and we look together. If getting ready is not important to them, it comes off and the day shrinks honestly. If the first dance matters, it stays. The timeline does the negotiating, not me.

Two things I say plainly. First, a shorter day costs less because they are buying less. What they keep costs what it costs, and the rate does not move because the day got shorter. I say that as a fact, not an apology. Second, the hours I would protect last are the ones around the ceremony and the two hours before sunset, because those are the ones they will miss if they are gone. The morning and the late dance floor are the honest places to trim.

What I never do: cut the price for the same scope, or let the couple cut coverage I know they will regret without telling them. If they cut it anyway after hearing me, that is their call and I book it cheerfully.

## My position on discounting

I do not discount. Not because I am precious about the number, but because a discount tells the couple the first number was not real, and every couple they refer to me will arrive expecting the same conversation. Here is the whole position.

Change the scope, never the price. If a couple cannot reach the number, the honest move is a smaller day, fewer photographers or a smaller deliverable, quoted at full rate. The same work for less money is the one thing I will not do.

Add rather than subtract. When I want to be generous, and I often do, I add value. An engagement session with a booking made by a date. A print credit. Extra time on a session. Generosity that adds something costs me hours, not margin, and it never resets the price in anyone's head.

Credits, not discounts. If I have promised a gift that the couple's booking already includes, I convert it to a credit toward something else rather than knocking money off. A gift that quietly becomes a discount teaches the wrong lesson.

Never discount the album, never gift it. In my ladder the album is the product with real cost of goods and a thin margin. [Name the equivalent in yours.] Giving it away or cutting it is the fastest way to turn a profitable booking into a loss while feeling generous.

Spread, never front-load. If I ever structure a payment concession, it is across the schedule, not a cheap first payment. A small deposit invites an easy yes and an easy cancellation. The deposit is what makes the date real for both of us.

Hold the price long enough to learn. When I change my prices I hold them for a set number of consults before I touch them again, [twenty] in my case, and I write the number down before the first call. The reversal trigger is written down in advance: the number of price objections per ten consults, each one logged in the couple's exact words. If the log fills, the price is wrong and I move it. If the log stays empty while the close rate climbs past seven in ten, the price is low and I move it the other way. Either way the decision comes from the log, not from how the last call felt.

## Asking for the decision

A close is one clear question after fit is established and concerns are resolved. "From everything we've talked through, I think [direction] is the right shape for your day. Would you like me to send the proposal for that?" Then silence. I do not fill it, I do not add a feature, I do not apologise for asking.

If they say yes, I stop selling. I confirm the direction, [your retainer], and what happens next. People who have decided do not want to be sold to.

If a concern appears, I resolve it, check that it is resolved, and ask once more. If a second, different concern appears, I address that too. If they clearly decline or ask for space, I stop. I agree a follow-up only if they want one. The respectful re-ask rule is simple: one ask per resolved concern, and never through silence.

## After the call

Three lines in the notes, every time. Where they landed. The exact words of any price objection, dated. One thing I will do differently next call. The first builds the follow-up. The second builds the pricing decision. The third builds the skill.
